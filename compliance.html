GST due dates, late fee and interest

The figures an officer or a taxpayer reaches for most often, in one place, with the provision or notification each one rests on.

Verify before you rely on this. Due dates are extended by notification more often than any other thing in GST, and rates and caps have changed several times. Everything below is stated with the provision or notification it comes from so you can check it. For any live case, confirm the position against cbic-gst.gov.in and the notifications in force for the period. This page is a reference, not advice.

Extensions are common and they matter. A due date extended by notification and not accounted for makes a return look late when it was not, and charges interest and late fee on days that were never delay. Two examples: the April 2022 GSTR-3B was moved to 24 May 2022 by Notification No. 05/2022-Central Tax after the portal failed, and the September 2022 GSTR-3B to 21 October 2022 by Notification No. 21/2022-Central Tax. Always check the period you are working on.

2. Late fee under Section 47

Section 47(1) provides for one hundred rupees per day per Act, subject to five thousand rupees. The daily rate was reduced by Notification No. 76/2018-Central Tax, and the ceiling was replaced with turnover-based caps by Notification No. 19/2021-Central Tax for tax periods from June 2021 onwards.

The slabs run from the tax period, not the due date. Notification No. 19/2021-CT applies “for the tax periods June, 2021 onwards”. The May 2021 return falls due on 20 June 2021, but it is a May period — so the older Rs. 5,000 per Act ceiling applies to it, not the turnover cap. Getting this the wrong way round understates the fee on every pre-June-2021 period.

3. Late fee on the annual return

Section 47(2) is a separate provision with a separate ceiling: one hundred rupees per day per Act, capped at a quarter per cent of turnover in the State or Union territory — a percentage, not a flat figure. Notification No. 07/2023-Central Tax reduced both the rate and the cap by turnover slab, for FY 2022-23 onwards.

GSTR-9 — FY 2022-23 onwards (Notification No. 07/2023-CT)

When is the annual return complete? Circular No. 246/03/2025-GST dated 30 January 2025 clarifies that where FORM GSTR-9C is required, the annual return is not complete until both GSTR-9 and GSTR-9C have been furnished. The late fee therefore runs continuously from the due date to the later of the two filing dates — it is not two separate fees.

Notification No. 08/2025-Central Tax dated 23 January 2025 waived the excess late fee for financial years up to FY 2022-23 where GSTR-9C was furnished on or before 31 March 2025. Check whether that waiver covers the year in hand before pressing the fee.

4. Interest under Section 50

SituationRateProvision
Delayed payment of tax18% per annums.50(1), Notification No. 13/2017-CT

Undue or excess reduction of output tax liability24% per annums.50(3) (pre-amendment position)

Interest runs on the cash portion only. Under the proviso to Section 50(1) read with Rule 88B(1), where the return for a period is furnished after the due date, interest is payable on that portion of the tax paid by debiting the electronic cash ledger — not on the amount discharged through credit already lying in the ledger. This single point is the most common error in an interest computation.

And under Rule 88B(3), credit wrongly availed attracts interest only where it has been utilised; credit taken and reversed without utilisation does not.

6. Time limit for taking credit

Section 16(4) sets the last date for taking input tax credit on an invoice or debit note.

Financial yearLast dateNote
FY 2017-18 to FY 2020-2130 November 2021 Section 16(5), inserted by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 — credit taken in any GSTR-3B furnished up to that date is admissible
FY 2021-2230 November 2022 The Finance Act 2022 substitution took effect on 1 October 2022, before the unamended date of 20 October 2022 had expired
FY 2022-23 onwards30 November following the financial year Or the date of furnishing the annual return, whichever is earlier

Sections 16(5) and 16(6) are a real defence. For the four years to FY 2020-21 a demand resting on time-bar alone will not hold, and Section 16(6) restores credit for a registered person whose registration was cancelled and later revoked. Both were inserted with retrospective effect. Consider them before raising any Section 16(4) demand.

The other conditions in Section 16(2)

  • (a) possession of a tax invoice or debit note
  • (aa) the details have been furnished by the supplier and appear in the recipient's GSTR-2B — the condition that makes GSTR-2B, and not GSTR-2A, the statement to test credit against from 1 January 2022
  • (b) the goods or services have been received
  • (c) the tax has actually been paid to the Government
  • (d) the return under Section 39 has been furnished

Rule 42 is a computation, not a ratio applied to everything. It requires credit to be segregated first — exclusively for non-business use, exclusively for exempt supply, blocked under Section 17(5), and exclusively for taxable supply — before the common credit is arrived at and apportioned. Applying the exempt-turnover ratio to the whole of Table 4(A) overstates the reversal wherever credit is exclusively attributable to taxable supplies. It is a useful risk indicator; it is not the statutory working.

8. Scrutiny, and what follows it

StageFormTimeProvision
Discrepancy intimated to the registered personASMT-10 —s.61(1), r.99(1)

Explanation found acceptable — no further actionASMT-12 —s.61(2), r.99(3)

Which determination provision applies. Section 73 (no fraud) and Section 74 (fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression) govern up to FY 2023-24. From FY 2024-25 onwards, Section 74A replaces both with a common limitation and a graded penalty. Getting the demarcation wrong is a common and fatal drafting error.

There is no materiality threshold in Section 61 or Rule 99. Every discrepancy is to be reported; what is worth pursuing is for the proper officer to decide.
